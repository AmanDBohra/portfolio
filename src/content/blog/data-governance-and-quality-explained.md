Everyone wants insights. Almost nobody gets excited about **data governance.** But it's the reason anyone can trust the insights in the first place — and it's simpler than it sounds.

## The core problem

No data makes you cautious. **Bad data makes you confident — and wrong.** A dashboard looks authoritative, so the room moves on and acts on it. The cost shows up later, in decisions built on sand. Governance exists to prevent that.

## Data quality, in six everyday checks

"Quality data" just means data that passes a few common-sense tests:

- **Complete** — nothing important is missing.
- **Accurate** — it reflects reality.
- **Consistent** — "West", "west ", and "WEST" are all the same thing.
- **Valid** — an age isn't 999; a date isn't in the future.
- **Unique** — no accidental duplicate records.
- **Timely** — fresh enough for the decision.

*Like proofreading a document for typos, blanks, and contradictions before you send it to the board.*

## Governance, in plain language

Governance is simply the **rules and checks** that keep data trustworthy across a company — who owns each dataset, how it's defined, and what has to be true before it's used.
*Traffic rules — so everyone moves safely and predictably instead of chaos.*

The single most useful habit is **maker/checker**: one person prepares a change, a second reviews and approves it before it reaches production.
*Like a co-pilot double-checking the checklist — one mistake shouldn't reach the runway.*

## Governance as a habit, not a project

The version that actually works isn't a giant committee. It's small, boring, and *built into delivery*: validate at the source, cleanse inside the pipeline, define each metric once, and track a simple data-quality score over time.

On enterprise engagements, applying these habits is how I improved **data accuracy by 35%** and **reduced audit findings by 15%** — not through a big program, but through checks baked into how the data was built.

## The takeaway

Treat trust as a feature of the deliverable, equal to the chart itself. The real question isn't only *"is this insightful?"* — it's *"would I stake a decision on this number?"* If the honest answer is no, the work isn't finished.
